> open casket

The lock of the casket quietly clicked open, and I lifted the lid curious as to what I would find.

As the diary had suggested, the casket contained a selection of papers and notes - including the missing pages that had been torn from my Uncle's journal.  I began to read....

I must confess that I didn't really understand much of what I read.  It seemed that my Uncle had inherited the music box, just as I had.  It also seems that it held a fascination for him as it did for me.  He had attempted to research its roots, and recorded things that puzzled and disturbed me.

He claimed that the music box had first come into our possession during the 17th century.  Our title and land comes from James Hansom, a privateer who plundered shipping and ports in the New World, all in the name of Charles II.  His success earned him much favour and when he returned to England it was as a rich man.  My Uncle wrote that he had discovered a strange story about James Hansom's first dealings with the box.

Apparently, James had purchased the box at a market in Genoa sometime in the 1670s.  My Uncle drew particular attention to this point, writing that this was unusual in itself, for the art of making such trinkets had not really developed until this very century.  The story went that James assumed that it did something, for it appeared to be mechanical, but was most vexed by the fact that it - in fact - appeared to do nothing.  Allegedly he took it to a craftsman in London, asking for him to take a look at it and fix it if he could.

About a week later the box was returned to James, with an angry letter from the craftsman stating that there was nothing wrong with the box.  He wrote that it appeared to be a quite ingenious mechanism that simply involved small pins hitting bells that resulted in a charming tune being played when the box was open.  In other words, there was nothing wrong with the box and he would thank Mr. Hansom to not waste his time in the future.

But James could still not get the box to work.  Two weeks later, he travelled to London himself to speak with the craftsman.  Upon reaching the craftsman's workshop he was astonished to find that three days before his arrival, the artificer had been taken by a fit of lunacy and had thrown himself from the second floor of his home, sustaining fatal injuries.

James thought nothing of it, other than it was a tragic event, and took the box to a different craftsman.  This craftsman declared that, whilst amazingly intricate and clever, the device appeared to be broken beyond repair.  It seems that following this, the box vanished until my Uncle found it amongst some belongings that he inherited.


[Press a Key]


Apparently, my Uncle thought it was nothing more than a beautiful piece for - like James before him - he could not get it to work.  Then one day a friend of his was visiting and opened the box.  He declared it to be playing the most wonderful tune that he had ever heard but my Uncle, who was in the room at the time, claimed that he heard nothing.  A week later my Uncle's friend leapt into the River Tees for no apparent reason and was drowned.

At this point, it appears that my Uncle began to suspect that the music box was not all that it appeared to be.  He began to write more and more frantic missives on his research.  He could find nothing about the origins of the box, but he became more and more convinced that it was cursed.  Eventually, he came to believe that those who heard it play music were doomed to insanity in the near future.

He became obsessed and decided to try and rid himself of the box.  He tells of a time when he travelled to Dover to take a ship to France.  Whilst in the middle of the English Channel he claims that he pitched the box overboard and watched it sink beneath the waves.  Yet, he also wrote that when he returned home from his trip, the box was sitting on his dining room table as though nothing had happened.

Perhaps the most disturbing record was the final page that he had clearly torn from his journal - dated just days before he took his own life.

"My worst fears have been confirmed.  I have decided to take all of my notes and lock them - along with the wretched box - inside my trick casket.  God willing, that will be enough for now.  I know that I have not much time, for this morning I heard the music."


[Press a Key]


I was pondering these notes for some time.  I was not sure what to make of them.  Was there really something sinister about the music box that I had found, or was this simply proof of my Uncle's deteriating mental state.  It certainly seemed like a story that was too fantastic to be true, and God knows that my Uncle had not been himself for some time.

I do not know how long I had been alone in the attic, but I was eventually brought out of my stupour by the sound of someone calling my name.  It was my brother.  He had returned from town.  I clambered out of the attic and met him on the stairs, where he had been ascending to find me.  I noticed, as he came up the stairs, that in his hand he held the music box that I had left in the kitchen.  It was open.  He smiled at me.

"Rupert, where did you find this exquisite toy?  Did it belong to Uncle John?  Isn't it playing the most delightful tune that you have ever heard?"

My heart almost stopped right there on those stairs.  For I, and I swear on my own life that this is true, I could hear no music.

There was silence in the room as Rupert concluded his story.  It was a most uncomfortable silence, for not a man of us did not know that his brother was in the Bedlam across town.  He had been taken by insanity a few years ago.  The silence was clearly too much for Huntsley to bear, and he was the first to break it.

"Rupert...surely you don't think...listen, your brother..."

"My brother is dead." Rupert snapped.  "This morning I was called to the Sanitorium to see his body.  He died in his sleep last night.  The doctors do not know what happened, but I saw his face.  It was twisted in terror.  He died of fear.  That box took his mind and then it took his life last night."

Silence again, only broken by the dull rhythm of the clock on the wall.  After about a minute, I spoke.  I asked the question that all of us had been thinking.

"What did you do with the box?"

Rupert seemed to slump in his chair, defeated.

"What could I do?  When my brother was taken ill I feared that my Uncle was right.  But what choice did I have?  I could not, in good conscience, sell it or pass it on.  Neither could I discard it.  If my Uncle was right then not even throwing it into the ocean would rid me of it.  Somehow it had found its way out of the casket so that I would find it that morning in the attic.  Perhaps I could destroy it, but how?  I knew that I could not find a blacksmith in the world who could be convinced to melt the damned thing down into silver and then discard it as though it were worthless.  Who would do that but a madman?  No.  I only had one option.  I locked it back in that casket and buried it under rags in the attic."

This time it was the Reverend who asked the question.

"And...is it still there?"

"I assumed that it was," Rupert answered, "Until I returned from visiting my brother's corpse this morning.  You can probably imagine my horror when I returned home and saw that."

He gestured to the fireplace on the far wall, and the air in the room chilled as we all looked.

There, on the mantel above the fire, previously unnoticed by any of us was a small, beautiful, silver box.  It was some time before any of us looked away, and again it was Huntsley who spoke first.

"Surely this is just a jest, Rupert?  Just a captial joke at our expense?"

"You think this is a fanciful jape?" Hansom raised his eyebrow.  "Well, go and open the box.  Surely at least one of you gentlemen is in the mood for a little music."

Unsurprisingly, none of us were.
