You're back in Michael's office.  It's last week.

"Tom," he says, "how are you getting on?  How have you been sleeping?"

"I've been sleeping well," you reply.

It's the truth, but it's an incomplete truth.  Most of the time an incomplete
truth is easier than a lie.  Don't tell him that you've been sleeping well,
but that when you wake up the furniture has been moved, papers scattered.
Don't tell him about the phone conversations.  And for God's sake, don't tell
him that you've stopped taking the Xanax.

Michael nods.

"Listen Tom.  I've been... well, we've all been a bit worried about you
lately.  I know this is a stressful project and there's a lot of interest
from outside sources that, well, we'd rather kept their noses out of our
business, but... I wonder if it's been a bit too much for you.  I wonder if
we've been pushing you too hard."

Michael studies your face, looking for a reaction, something that might give
him a clue as to what's going on inside you.  You stay silent and passive.
You forget how long you've been silent and passive.

"We think you should take a couple of weeks off.  Take a break.  Maybe go and
visit your parents or something.  Do something relaxing to take your mind off
work."

You nod.  It has been a while since you've seen mum and dad.  The thought of
going to visit them seems to stir something within you.  Something you would
rather remained buried.

"I'm OK," you try. "I'm fine.  I'm enjoying this project.  I think I'm coping
well."

Michael is once again staring intently at you.

"Tom.  Are you still taking your medication?"

"Yes."

Sometimes the lie is easier than the incomplete truth.

You're back.  In the kitchen.  You're six years old.  It's been about a year
since Howard died.  Mum is screaming at you again.  You don't recall what
it's about.  It doesn't seem to matter.  Muddy footprints; forgetting to
flush the toilet; putting a plate back in the wrong place.  Anything and
everything is a minefield.  Your life is one of intense volume, both inside
and out.

Your mother's red face and screaming mouth becomes your whole world.  Yet,
out of the corner of your eye you see your father in the background.  Head
bowed, silent, impotent as always.

Tears streaming down your face, you lash out.

"I hate you!  I hate both of you!  You killed Howard!"

Instantly you regret your rebellion and ill-chosen words.  Your mother's
crimson cheeks become as white as porcelain, but only for a second.  Then
they flush even deeper and the next thing you know she has you by the hair.

She's dragging you towards the garden.  You're kicking and pleading with her
to let go.  Your father follows, mumbling something in your defense.

She's got you at the coal bunker.  You're begging for her to stop and you're
telling her that you're sorry and that you'll never do anything like that
again.  She's telling you that after this you will never speak to her like
that again.  She hurls you.

Then darkness.  The sound of doors being locked.  The awareness of not having
enough room to stretch out.  The jagged lumps of coal digging into your side.

"We'll see if you've learnt some manners by tomorrow morning." Your mother's
harsh voice breaks through the sound of your own screams, speaking words that
are seared onto the soul of a child. "The only person that you should blame
for Howard's death is yourself."

After that, your howls and pounding are only met with silence.

You're back.  On your knees in the garden.

The lifeless expressions of your mother and father look up at you from the
ground, faces smeared with coal dust.  Lying beside them, sandwiched between
two lumps of coal, is a book.  Your diary.  The diary in which you recorded
all your memories, shame, hurt and fear.  The diary you hid in your room so
that your parents would never find it.

"I hate dad," it says on one page.  On another, "I hate mum."

They can't hurt you any more.  They can't haunt you when you close your eyes
and see only the pitch black of the coal bunker.  They can't torment you with
their weakness and anger and guilt any longer.  They are gone.  You tell
yourself this again and again.

But the angry wound within you still throbs, and calls you a liar.

You are back.  Back home.
