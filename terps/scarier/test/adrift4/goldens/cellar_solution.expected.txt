From the Lovecraft Commonplace Book:

"Man's body dies - but
corpse retains life. Stalks
about - tries to conceal
odour of decay - detained
somewhere - hideous climax."

"And you're never to go into the cellar, Nevare!"

"Yes, father."

"Never, you understand! If I ever find you've gone down there-"

"Yes, father."

"Good, Nevare. I knew I could trust you. You're a good boy."

But now your father is away on business and you're all alone in the house.
And you know where he keeps the key to the cellar.

l l l Father's Room l l l

A sturdy oak table has been placed in the dead centre of your father's room,
its weight enough to make the floorboards creak and groan late at night.
There are papers scattered across the table's surface, each covered with your
father's tight, meticulous writing. A bed beneath the window is where he
sleeps; it seems small, much too small for his large frame. A door to the
east leads to the hallway at the top of the stairs. In an alcove in one wall
are your father's collection of journals.

In a box under the bed, that's where your father keeps the key. You should
grab it and quickly. He won't return for many hours... but even so, it never
pays to take chances.

l l l Hidden Chamber l l l

This room, unknown to you before today, is lit so poorly as to make seeing
anything beyond the end of your nose a trying task. Nevertheless, you can
discern the outline of a bed in the centre of the room, and perhaps a row of
cabinets around it, but it is the... the thing lying on the bed that draws
your attention. Seen barely in the dismal light, it is a grotesque mockery of
a man, bloated and distorted, pale flesh spotted with tufts of coarse hair.

The thing stirs. It's too dark to tell for certain, but you feel, or sense,
it turning to look at you.

"Ah... a visitor..." The voice is a croak. A harsh whisper. A pained grunt.
"So rare these days. Step forward, boy. Let me see what you look like."

You don't obey. Not consciously at least. You're too terrified. Yet something
in the thing's voice compels you and you take a step forward all the same.

"Well, well," it murmurs. "This is a pleasant surprise." The thing leans
forward slightly and its face, barely recognisable as human, moves into the
light. "Hello, nephew."

You stare in dismay at your Uncle Gavin.

"It is true, every word of it. I see the doubt in your eyes, Nevare. Good.
You're a clever boy to not accept everything at face value. Only a fool

"Only a fool would end up like I have done. Tell me, boy, do you believe in

The question startles you. The sheer… absurdity of it. And no, you're eleven
years old. You've never considered such things.

"You should understand there are things in this world that defy
comprehension, Nevare. There are things… things that I, even looking as I do
now, even after having undergone the… the transformation that I have
undergone… things that I fear. I came across one such being on the expedition
with your father and Penwright and Clarkson and Simonson. I came face to face
with evil in its purest form. What you see now, nephew,"  a mottled hand
gestures to the rest of the thing lying on the bed, "is what happened to me
because of it. I looked evil in the face and evil looked back at me."

There are moments of silence following this, moments which build into
minutes. The thing on the bed - your uncle, you remind yourself, but the
image you have in your mind of Gavin Crayson is so different from what you
see before you that you cannot reconcile the two - breathes in and out,
painfully it seems. It exudes a stench which makes your nose curl and your
stomach groan, yet you keep any outward expression from showing. Not through
manners, though.

Through fear.

"I shall tell you what happened to me, Nevare," says the thing/your uncle.
"It is right that you know. Your father… he wanted to launch a second
expedition when the first one failed. Despite what had befallen his only
brother, he wanted to go back out there. One day, when you're older, he'll
probably invite you. Look at me, boy. LOOK AT ME! And when he invites you,
tell him no! Tell him! Lest you end up like me."  Your uncle's face looms
briefly into the light then recedes again. "But first… first I desire…
sustenance… Go to the kitchen… Find me something to… something to eat,
Nevare. Something... palatable..." The thing laughs harshly. "Something I
would find palatable. Go..."

The voice trails off, becoming weaker and quieter, until you suspect your
uncle has fallen asleep.

You creep from the chamber.

Uncle Gavin's Story
{part 1}

The four of us continued at a good pace. I led, your father brought up the
rear, and Simonson and Clarkson walked between, talking quietly amongst
themselves. They seemed ill-inclined to continue and many times I heard them
mention the idea of simply turning back.

That night we made camp in a dried river bed. We ate a meal of beef jerky and
washed it down with water which tasted foul, even though we had drawn it
fresh from a river that very morning. Simonson insisted this was because the
land was cursed and that we, continuing as we did, were cursed also. Your
father told him not to be a fool, that there was no such thing as curses and
then Clarkson, who I had never known to argue with your father before, said
that he had spoken to a coloured man in Jiman who had forecast our expedition
would fail and that Penwright would be the first of us to die. None of us
were really sure what to make of that and so your father ordered Clarkson to
desist in his foolishness. Nothing more was said on the subject.

We slept poorly that night. I perhaps managed an hour but every time I seemed
about to slip off, something awoke me. A sound, a perceived movement, a
feeling that something was wrong.

I awoke in an ill temper the next day but my anger quickly turned to dread
when only three of us crawled from our beddings. When your father nudged
Clarkson to see if he was merely a heavier sleeper than we had assumed, we
discovered he was dead.

Uncle Gavin's Story
{part 2}

We buried Clarkson in the river bed as best we could, piling stones atop him.
Simonson raised the issue again that we should turn back and I was all for
agreeing with him, but your father was obstinate that we continue. He said
that to turn back now would make the sacrifices of Penwright and Clarkson in

'sacrifices' but didn't wish to cause friction between your father and
Simonson so I held my peace. In the end, as I knew would happen, Simonson
bowed to your father's determined stance and we pressed on.

The weather turned cold. Frighteningly cold. We had ill prepared for it,
assuming that Africa, in the height of summer, would be a hot place. We
suffered for our poor planning. Again, Simonson raised the issue of us
turning back and he and your father came close to shouting at each other on
many occasions. I acted as a go between, though my feelings on the subject
were that turning back would be a good idea indeed. I think the only thing
that kept me from doing so was that I had always admired your father, Nevare,
and had sought for a good part of my adult life to emulate him. To be him, if
you will. Turning back - worse still, siding with Simonson against your
father - would have been an admission that I wasn't cut from the same cloth
as he was. So I acted as the go between to calm down tempers and in that way
we pressed on further.

We should have reached the village the following day. It was marked on our
maps, unclearly but marked all the same, yet we reached the area where it was
reputed to lie and found ourselves in a dusty valley. If a village had ever
stood anywhere within that valley, we could find no trace of it.

Yet the valley was not entirely deserted. There was one person waiting for us
when we arrived at its far end and his presence placed a cold chill in my
heart.

It was Penwright.

Uncle Gavin's Story
{part 3}

Yes, Penwright. But… changed.

He had always been a large fellow, hefty and strong, yet now he seemed larger
still. He must have been seven feet tall, and his shoulders bulged more
massively than ever. But it was his complexion, his very skin, which chilled
me. It was grey. And cold. His eyes were lifeless orbs.

He spoke to us. I don't recall all the words. He told us that we were fools
to come here, that the secrets we sought were not ours to know and that
instead of life eternal we would be cursed with death if we did not turn
back.

Simonson dropped his pack and fled. I suspect I would have done the same yet
the appearance of a man I knew full well to be dead froze me on the spot. I
could not move.

Your father, incredibly, took a step towards the living corpse. He said, "we
are here and we demand what is rightfully ours." I remember the words well.
They were the words which damned me.

The thing that was Penwright but which was now something… different… took a
step towards your father. It lifted an arm and pointed at him. It said, "go."

Your father stood firm.

The thing seemed to laugh at him. At least I gather that the hideous croaking
sound was a laugh. It then said, "then you shall suffer the consequences."

But when the thing pointed with its gnarled finger, it was not your father it
pointed at.

It was me.
